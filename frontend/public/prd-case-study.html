The stuff you save to get better at your job, and never look at again.

How I designed and built an AI tool for the things you save to learn from. The first version is for people studying product management, but nothing underneath it is tied to that.

Everyone saves things they mean to learn from. Almost nobody goes back. PM Content Vault takes the posts, PDFs, screenshots and chat exports sitting in five different apps, works out what each one is, and lets you ask questions of the whole pile, with every answer showing the saves it came from.

The problem

Anyone learning on their own builds up a pile of saved stuff. A framework from LinkedIn. A PDF dropped in a group chat. A screenshot. An article bookmarked at midnight. All saved on purpose, almost none of it opened again.

The easy answer is that people are messy and need better folders. I don't buy it. Saving isn't the broken part. Three things break after it.

It's scattered

LinkedIn, WhatsApp, bookmarks, a notes app. No one place to look, so looking anywhere feels pointless.

You can't judge it

A saved link is a title and a thumbnail. Finding out if it's worth ten minutes takes ten minutes.

You can't find it

You remember "that post about handling stakeholder conflict". That's never a word that actually appears in it.

The idea this product is built on

Storing things was never the problem. Finding them and judging them was. That's why every item gets a summary the moment it's saved, and why search came before tidying features.

Who it's for

Long term, anyone who saves things to learn from: exam takers, career switchers, people teaching themselves something new. The habit is the same everywhere.

The first group is people learning product management. The problem isn't special to them, but they share a vocabulary, a deadline and predictable content, which makes the categories worth having and the users easy to find.

Why start narrow when the problem is wide

A fixed set of categories is what makes a saved pile scannable, and you can only pick good ones if you know the subject. Build for everyone on day one and you get vague categories, which pushes the sorting work back onto the user. That's the thing that already failed. Opening it up later is a settings change, not a rebuild.

Why build it when so much already exists

Lots of tools store saved content. None fix what was actually broken.

What I could have usedWhy it doesn't fit
A notes appGreat storage, but sorting is your job, and that's the step people skip mid-scroll.
A read-later appBuilt for a reading queue. It answers "what haven't I read", not "where's that thing about stakeholder conflict".
A general AI chatbotAnswers questions about a file you hand it, but every chat starts empty. Nothing builds up.
Platform searchSaved posts stay on the app that hosted them. Five searches instead of one, all keyword-only.
The gap this fills

Each option is good at one of three things: take anything, understand it without being asked, answer across all of it with sources. The value is doing all three to the same pile, and they depend on each other.

What it does

How it's organised

Five places, plus two decisions underneath.

Dashboard : counts, recent saves, continue a conversation Library : everything, filtered by category / source / date / duplicates └ Item : the content, its summary, highlights, related items Ask My Vault : conversations, each answer linked back to the Library Favorites : starred items, with study actions over them Settings : account, defaults, data

One message is one item

A WhatsApp export doesn't save as one item. Each message becomes its own, because a 40-message chat with three good ideas in it is otherwise one unsearchable blob. Splitting it up front is what lets a source link point at something small enough to check.

Two kinds of label, on purpose

CategoriesTags
Who sets themThe AI picks from a fixed list of six. You can change it, or type one of your ownNothing free-form yet. The favourite star is the only tag the app writes
How manyOne to three per item, never zeroOne, favorite
WhyHolding the model to six keeps filtering reliable. You aren't held to them, because your filing shouldn't depend on my guess about your subjectA general table instead of a favorite column, so tags can grow later without a migration
Never zero

Every item gets at least one category, falling back to Other. An "uncategorised" pile sounds harmless, but it's where things go to be forgotten, and forgetting is the problem this product exists to fix.

Decisions that shaped it

The feature list is the least interesting part. What got argued down is where the thinking is.

Decision 01

Search by meaning, not keywords, and no model training

Keyword search fails on the query people actually have, because you remember a post's idea, not its words. Training a model has a different problem: your library changes daily, so every save means retraining. Search by meaning solves both, and every answer can point back at what it came from. That last part became the tiebreaker for every later call.

Decision 02

Nothing is saved until you confirm

The normal way is to save the row first and tidy it in the background. I did the opposite: the AI work runs in memory, and only pressing save writes anything. An abandoned add leaves nothing behind, and a duplicate warning is only useful before you save. Telling someone they've just made a duplicate is a notification, not a feature.

Decision 03

No LinkedIn scraping, even though it's the biggest source

Most of what I save is on LinkedIn, and auto-importing it would be the single most useful feature. It also breaks LinkedIn's terms, so the app only takes a pasted link or text. I wrote it into the brief as a locked rule before building, so I couldn't talk myself out of it at 1am when the manual flow felt slow.

Decision 04

Cutting a feature I'd already planned

"Resurfacing" was feature #7 in my own brief: anything untouched for 14 days pops up in a reminder widget. Six of seven shipped. I cut this one, and not because it was hard. It was the cheapest thing left, two rules over a date. I cut it because showing you something just because it's old is a guess about what you want, and there's no evidence for it. It goes back on the list the day users show me otherwise.

Decision 05

A hard $0 budget, treated as a design constraint

Everything runs on a free tier, and text extraction happens in the browser where it costs nothing. That shaped the build, not just the bill. Uploads cap at 4MB because the hosting rejects anything bigger before my code runs, so a higher limit would swap a clear message for a confusing failure. A daily ping keeps the database awake, since free projects sleep and a portfolio link is most likely to be opened by the person you least want waiting.

AI architecture

Two AI paths, kept apart on purpose: one runs when content arrives, the other when a question is asked. They share an embedding model and nothing else.

Ingestion: when content arrives
  1. Save content paste · upload · chat export
  2. ↓
  3. Content extraction HTML · OCR · PDF · chat parser
  4. ↓
  5. AI sorts it six fixed categories
  6. ↓
  7. AI summarises it gist · key points
  8. ↓
  9. Embeddings 768 dimensions
  10. ↓
  11. Vector database pgvector · dedup check
Retrieval: when a question is asked
  1. Ask My Vault question embedded, top matches retrieved
  2. ↓
  3. Grounded answer + citations only the sources the answer used

What each stage does

  • Reading depends on the type. Notes arrive as clean HTML, screenshots go through Tesseract in the browser, PDFs through pdf-parse, chat exports split one item per message.
  • Sorting and summarising happen in one call. Gemini returns a category, title, summary and 3 to 5 key points. The reply is checked, not trusted: broken JSON gets repaired, and an empty reply throws instead of quietly saving a blank item.
  • The duplicate check reuses the same maths, and runs before saving, while it can still change your mind.
  • Only sources the answer actually used get listed. Search returns the top matches and the answer uses some. Listing them all would make "used 5 sources" a lie.

Designing for the model failing

The model is treated as unreliable, because it is.

What goes wrongWhat happens
Model overloadedRetry, then fall back to other models, cheapest first, since the best one allows about 20 requests a day free
Out of daily quotaA different message, because the advice differs: wait a minute versus wait until tomorrow
Broken reply formatRepaired field by field instead of thrown away
The rule underneath

All of these fall back to the same thing: you still get your content saved. The one thing that must never slip is the source link. An answer you can't check is worse than no answer, because it looks exactly like one you can.

What I'd measure, and what I'd ask

Set before launch, so success can't be defined afterwards to match the numbers. Each one names the failure it would catch, not just the thing it counts.

The four numbers I care about

QuestionMeasureWhy this one
Does saved content get used?% of saves opened at least once in 30 daysThe main claim. If this doesn't beat roughly zero, nothing else matters.
Does search actually work?% of questions where someone opens a sourceAn answer nobody clicks into is just a nice-sounding answer. The failure I fear most.
Is it faster than before?Time from question to the right saveIt only earns its place if it beats scrolling LinkedIn saves.
Is the AI trusted?% of AI-filled fields edited before savingLots of edits means it's getting things wrong. Zero probably means nobody's reading them.

The five questions I'd ask users

  1. Show me the last five things you saved. Where are they? (Is "scattered" real?)
  2. When did you last go looking for something you'd saved? What did you do?
  3. What happened when you couldn't find it? (Googling it again means the save was wasted.)
  4. Here's an answer built from three of your saves. Do you trust it? What would make you check?
  5. You haven't opened this in three weeks. Should the app have said so? (Decides whether resurfacing comes back.)
What would change my mind

If three of five describe hunting for something they knew they'd saved and giving up, resurfacing goes back in, triggered by what they were looking for and not by how old the item is. If nobody opens a source, the trust model is wrong.

Product Requirements: MVP

The brief the build ran against, written before the first line of code.

1. Problem

Saved learning material scatters across LinkedIn, group chats and bookmarks and rarely gets opened again (full version above). What it means for the build: summaries are written at save time instead of on demand, and "find the right thing fast" matters more than "store everything".

2. Who it's for

MVP: people learning product management, whose content is predictable enough to make a fixed category list work. Long term: anyone who saves things to learn from. Not yet: other subjects, which is a categories job and not a rebuild (reasoning above).

3. Goals and non-goals

GoalsNon-goals
One place that takes every format this content arrives inA general read-later app
Judge if an item is worth reading, without reading itReplacing LinkedIn or WhatsApp
Answer questions across everything saved, with sourcesAnswering from general knowledge, not your saves
Catch duplicates before they pile upSharing or multi-user features

4. Rules locked before building

  • No LinkedIn scraping. Paste a link or the text only, since scraping breaks their terms.
  • No listening to WhatsApp. You export a chat yourself and upload the .txt, which gets split into items.
  • Single user for now. No sharing, but every table has an owner from day one so multi-user isn't a rewrite.
  • Resurfacing cut (2026-08-06, after the other six shipped). See Decision 04.

5. Feature scope

#FeatureStatus
1By hand: text, link, image (OCR), PDF, WhatsApp export, question, job postingShipped
2Sorting and summarising at save timeShipped
3Embeddings in pgvector, so search works by meaningShipped
4Library: list, filter, search, sortShipped
5Ask My Vault, every answer linked back to the saves it usedShipped
6Duplicate warnings when a new save is too close to an old oneShipped
7Resurfacing widgetCut, see Decision 04

6. Data model

TableWhat it holds
itemsThe content, its summary, key points, category, dates, and fields like page count
embeddingsThe maths behind search by meaning, one per item
item_categories, tagsAn item's one to three categories, and the tag table, which currently holds only favourites
duplicatesNear-identical pairs and how close they were
annotationsHighlights and notes, anchored so they survive edits
chat_sessions, chat_queriesConversations, answers, and the sources each one used

7. Categories

Interview Questions (Product Sense · RCA · Metrics · Strategy · Behavioural) · Job Postings · Application Tips · Frameworks · Industry News · Other.

The classifier is held to these six. You can add your own on any item, so the list is closed to the model and open to you.

8. What this project demonstrates

Defining a problem and rejecting the obvious answer, scoping an MVP with locked rules and one planned feature cut, building the search pipeline end to end, designing for trust and for the model failing, shipping it alone on a $0 stack.

9. Roadmap

None of this is built. Each says why it isn't in the MVP.

WhatWhy it matters
Any job, any subjectOnly the category list is specific to product management. Make it configurable, or infer it from what someone saves, and this becomes a general study tool.

Later: needs other people in it

WhatWhy it matters
Shared vaultsStudy groups already pass this material around in chats. A shared vault is where export and multi-user meet.
Anonymous "what's trending"The one network effect here: the embeddings that power personal search would group across accounts to show what everyone is studying. Needs multi-user and enough people to mean anything.
Why in this order

Next fixes what annoys people about something that already works. Then widens who it works for. Later needs other people signed up, so it can't be pulled forward however tempting.
